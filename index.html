A practical way to bring P.A.C.E. into a team

Use what fits your setting. P.A.C.E. is meant to be brief enough to survive an ordinary workday.

Facilitator quick start

  1. Choose the moment.Put P.A.C.E. at the start of a meeting, shift check-in, supervision, or another gathering that already exists.
  2. Choose four activities.One Presence, one Attune, one Connect, one Energy. Keep the total under 10 minutes.
  3. Tell people what to expect.Participation is voluntary. No personal explanation is required. People can skip an activity.
  4. Run the sequence.Read the instructions, do the activity, and move on. Processing is not required.
  5. Use what the room showed you.Before continuing the meeting, decide whether the plan still fits the room.

Two-week pilot

The ask

Five to ten minutes at the start of regular team meetings or shift check-ins for two weeks.

Keep it voluntary

No required disclosure. No emotional monitoring. No participation score. Stop if the practice is not useful.

Rotate the lead

A different person can choose the activities and run the sequence. The app carries the instructions.

The only question

After two weeks: Should we keep doing this? The optional check-in at the end of each sequence gives you a running answer without asking anyone to explain themselves.

How you will know

Success here is behavior you can see, not a feeling anyone has to report. P.A.C.E. never asks anyone to feel differently.

Signs it is working

Meetings start easier and closer to on time. Someone actually uses the pause signal, the stop time, or "drop something." On a low-capacity day the plan changes instead of the people pushing through. People keep joining without being chased, and eventually someone runs it without being asked.

Signs it is not

It feels like a chore the facilitator drags the room through. Most people skip most activities most days. Nothing about pace, workload, or stopping points ever changes. If that is where you land after two weeks, stop or change the format. That is the practice working as designed, not a failure.

How to introduce it without a speech

"We're going to try a short P.A.C.E. sequence at the start of this meeting. It takes a few minutes. You won't be asked to explain how you're feeling or share anything personal. Join in where it works for you, and skip anything that doesn't."

When the room gives you a problem

People do not want cameras on

Leave them off. Presence and many Attune and Energy activities work without video. For Connect, choose an audio, counting, or camera-free option from the full library.

You only have three minutes

Use the time filter. Pick 30-second and one-minute activities and keep the transition between them clean.

An activity does not fit this group

Replace it or skip it. Nobody needs to explain why.

The team thinks it feels awkward

Keep the first attempts short. P.A.C.E. does not require belief or enthusiasm. Run the activity, notice what is useful, and move on.

The group shows very low capacity

Do not turn that into a discussion about individual people. Use it to adjust the work: slow the pace, shorten the agenda, drop something nonessential, or set a stopping point.

Accessibility in the room

  • Offer camera-off participation
  • Read instructions aloud and keep them visible
  • Do not rely on color alone
  • Offer a non-movement option when movement is used
  • Let people skip without explanation
  • Do not make speed a measure of participation

About P.A.C.E.

What sits underneath the four steps

P.A.C.E. is a brief team practice for the start of work. One activity from Presence, Attune, Connect, and Energy. Most sequences take less than 10 minutes and do not require personal disclosure.

Why this order

P  Presence

Start with the immediate environment. External orientation gives people current information about where they are before the group asks anything more of them.

A  Attune

Notice the room's available pace or capacity without asking people to explain what is behind it.

C  Connect

Use brief shared timing, attention, or movement. Research on interpersonal synchrony links coordinated action with social connection and cooperation, while the mechanisms are still being studied.

E  Energy

End with resources and limits. Stress increases when time, energy, and other valued resources are threatened or depleted, so capacity has to affect how work is paced.

Research and practice

Attention and orientation

Where attention goes matters. Presence activities use ordinary, external information — the room, the floor, an object, the workspace — rather than asking people to turn inward or tell a story.

Coordination and synchrony

Shared timing and coordinated action have been studied across movement, rhythm, and group behavior. P.A.C.E. uses very short versions of those ideas without treating any one neuroscience explanation as settled fact.

Resources and workload

Conservation of Resources theory describes stress in part through threatened or depleted resources. Energy activities turn pace, workload, and stopping points into information the team can use.

Trauma-informed practice

Choice, collaboration, safety, transparency, and resistance to retraumatization shape the practice. That is why participation is voluntary, activities can be skipped, and P.A.C.E. does not collect individual wellness responses.

P.A.C.E. is a practice framework informed by these bodies of research. It is not presented as a clinical treatment or as a clinically validated intervention.

References

  1. Hobfoll, S. E. (1989). Conservation of resources: A new attempt at conceptualizing stress. American Psychologist, 44(3), 513–524. doi:10.1037/0003-066X.44.3.513.
  2. Mogan, R., Fischer, R., & Bulbulia, J. A. (2017). To be in synchrony or not? A meta-analysis of synchrony's effects on behavior, perception, cognition and affect. Journal of Experimental Social Psychology, 72, 13–20. doi:10.1016/j.jesp.2017.03.009.
  3. Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration. (2014). SAMHSA's Concept of Trauma and Guidance for a Trauma-Informed Approach (SMA 14-4884).
  4. P.A.C.E. training materials also draw on work by Stephen Porges, Deb Dana, Giacomo Rizzolatti, Joseph LeDoux, and Daniel Siegel. Those bodies of work are used as foundations and context rather than proof that a particular P.A.C.E. activity produces a specific clinical effect.

How teams know it is working

P.A.C.E. works when the group uses what it notices. The signs are visible in how work happens, so nobody has to report a feeling to prove anything.

  • Meetings start easier and closer to on time
  • Pause signals, stop times, and "drop something" get used for real
  • On low-capacity days the plan changes, not the people
  • Participation continues by choice, and others start to lead it

If none of that happens after a fair try, stopping is a legitimate outcome. The practice asks for a decision, not loyalty.

What P.A.C.E. does not require

  • Personal or emotional disclosure
  • Belief in the process
  • Cameras on for every activity
  • Individual regulation before the group begins
  • A wellness score or mood check
  • Tracking who participates

Accessibility

Built for different ways of participating

P.A.C.E. is designed to meet WCAG 2.2 Level AA accessibility standards.

In the app

  • Keyboard access and visible keyboard focus
  • Screen-reader labels, headings, landmarks, and status messages
  • Text and controls that reflow on smaller screens and at larger text sizes
  • Color is never the only way a step, choice, or status is identified
  • High-contrast text and controls
  • Large touch targets for primary controls
  • Reduced-motion support when the device requests it
  • Timers do not automatically move the group to the next activity

In the practice

  • Participation is voluntary
  • No personal disclosure is required
  • Many activities work with cameras off
  • Activities can be skipped without explanation

Accessibility is checked in the final production build as well as during design and development. If something in this app creates a barrier, contact info@traumainformedoregon.org.
